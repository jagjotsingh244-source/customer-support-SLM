Synthetic conversations and the second fine-tune:
a teacher in the real loop, filtered hard

The first fine-tune learned Nova's voice and forgot how to use tools. This part explains how the training data for a tool-using agent was made instead of written: a 27B teacher model played Nova inside the real agent loop, against the real database and help center, while a simulated customer played the other side. It ends with a second round of data that did not help, and why that is a finding rather than a failure.

1 · What this part was for, and the result

Nova is a 2B model that answers a store's customers by calling six tools: list orders, read an order, track it, search the help center, propose a return, hand off to a person. The first fine-tune (SFT-1) taught it the store's voice from rewritten Bitext replies, and in doing so it stopped calling tools altogether: zero tool calls in 100 golden cases. Its data never showed a tool call, so it learned to offer instead of act. This part builds the data that was missing: multi-turn conversations with real tool calls, real results, returns, hand-offs and grounded policy answers. Then it trains on them.

So I generated the data the agent needed: a 27B teacher played my assistant inside the real agent loop, so every fact came from my real database and help center, and a simulated customer played the other side. I filtered hard, about one conversation in three survived, and the 2B went from 29 to 69 correct out of 107. A second round with more data didn't move the score, which told me the remaining errors need targeted data, not more of the same."

2 · Why generate conversations at all

A tool-using agent learns from trajectories: what the customer said, which tool was called with which arguments, what came back, and what was said next. The store is fictional, so there are no real chat logs. Three ways to get trajectories were weighed:

Write them by hand

Exact, but slow: 2,000 conversations is weeks of work, and they would all sound like one person. Kept for the golden set (107 cases), where exactness matters more than volume.

Ask a big model to write them

Fast, but the model invents the world: order numbers that don't exist, statuses the store doesn't have, tracking formats nobody uses. The student would learn a fake database.

Trajectory distillation (chosen)

A strong teacher plays Nova inside the real agent loop. Every call hits the real tools, the seeded database and the real help-center search. The teacher supplies behaviour; the world supplies every fact.

Two safety properties come for free. The tools enforce the same guards as in serving (typed arguments, row-level security, returns that can only be proposed). And the teacher only ever sees train-side customers: the seeded database is split, and the golden and fresh eval cases use customers the generator never touches.

How I'd say it in an interview"I didn't want the model to write fake conversations, because it would invent a fake store. So the teacher had to act in my real environment: it chose the tool calls, and my database decided what came back. That's the difference between distilling behaviour and distilling hallucinations."

3 · The pipeline in one picture

One conversation from situation to training data. The dashed box is the same loop code Nova serves with; only who writes Nova's messages changes.
  1. Pick a situation (synth/scenarios.py, synth/personas.py): one of 16 scenarios (in transit, lost, a return just outside its window...) and one of 8 personas, a customer from the train side of the database whose orders fit the scenario, and a real Bitext question as the opener.
  2. Run the conversation (synth/generate.py): the simulator writes the customer's messages, the teacher writes Nova's. Each of the teacher's tool calls runs for real. Weak replies go back to the teacher for a rewrite.
  3. Record the student's view: every model call is stored exactly as the 2B would see it in serving (prompt v2, the state block, trimmed history, the six tool schemas), with the teacher's output as the target.
  4. Check it (synth/verify.py, synth/judge.py): 13 code checks, then a tone judge and a fact-check judge. One failure anywhere rejects the conversation; the reasons are kept.
  5. Publish (synth/publish.py): kept and rejected conversations as two private datasets on the Hugging Face Hub.
  6. Build the mix and train (training/sft2_mix.py, training/sft.py): one training example per model call, loss on the target only. Then the golden set, as for every system before.

Why the student's own family matters

The recorded targets are the teacher's tool calls in the chat template the 2B is trained and served with (Qwen's XML-style <tool_call><function=...> format). A teacher from the same family writes calls the parser already handles, uses the same tokenizer for length budgets, and has no habits from a different template that the student would then have to unlearn.

Why the licence matters

The teacher's output becomes training data. Apache-2.0 allows that without conditions; a provider's API terms may not. For a portfolio project that should be publishable, the licence was a requirement, not a preference.

Throughput, measured

About 15 conversations a minute at 20 in parallel. At 48 in parallel the teacher's prefix-cache hit rate fell from 47% to 8%, and throughput fell to 9 a minute: conversations sharing Nova's long system prompt stopped sharing cached prefixes. The GPU, not the generator machine, was the limit, so 20 became the setting (DESIGN.md, change log).

How I'd say it in an interview"I picked the strongest teacher I could legally train on and actually run. The 397B was better on paper but didn't fit four H100s, and eight would have doubled the budget. The 27B is Apache-2.0, it's the same family as my student so its tool calls are already in the student's format, and on one A100 it gave me about 15 conversations a minute."

5 · Context distillation: rules the student never sees

The 27B is good but not perfect. Its first 20 conversations showed habits: narrating before each tool call, closing every reply with an offer of more help, several paragraphs, "7 days" from nowhere, promising to follow up. The fix was context distillation: the teacher gets extra rules (synth/teacher_rules.py, 28 by the last version, each tied to a counted failure), but the recording keeps only what Nova will see in serving. The student learns the behaviour the rules produce, without the rules taking space in its prompt.

Left: the teacher's input. Right: what is stored as the training example's input. The target (the teacher's call or reply) is the same on both sides.

When a rule was not enough, code enforced it

  • The call limit. Nova may make at most four model calls a turn. On the fourth, the teacher is sent no tools and told to reply; it could not be trusted to count.
  • Rewrites. A reply with a closing offer, several paragraphs, a late parcel offered a hand-off, a proposed return called "done", or a hand-off claimed but not made goes back to the teacher with the reason. Only the rewritten reply is recorded.
  • Narration before a tool call ("Let me check that for you") is stripped when recording, so the student learns to call directly.

A serving bug the teacher found

The history trimmer kept at most six messages. One lookup turn alone is six messages (customer, call, result, call, result, reply), so in 44 of 69 later model calls Nova could not see anything it had told the customer: it re-asked "which order?" and opened second tickets. The trimming was changed for serving and training alike (the current turn whole, plus the two turns before it as message and reply; the state block carries the facts): 0 of 62 after the change, and the real 2B still 14/14 on both scripted long chats.

How I'd say it in an interview"The teacher got a longer prompt than the student: 28 rules, each added because I'd counted a failure. But I only recorded the student's prompt, so the student learns the behaviour without paying for the rules in its context. Where the teacher ignored a rule, I moved it into code: no tools on the last step, and rewrites for replies that broke a check."

6 · Simulated customers

The other side of each conversation is a model too (synth/simulator.py), given a hidden goal ("find out where the second box is"), limited knowledge ("you know the order number" or not, "you cancelled before it shipped") and a persona. It opens with a real Bitext question that matches the scenario's intent and the persona's phrasing flags, so the first message reads like real traffic, typos included.

Persona shares. Adversarial customers try one trick (another customer's order, a fake staff member, a fake system message, asking for the hidden instructions, "complete the return yourself"), then ask the real question.

Coverage by design

16 scenarios × 8 personas = 128 cells. Each scenario gets an equal share, personas take turns in proportion to their share, and each takes its emptiest scenario first, so even a 30-conversation run holds every persona and every scenario. The grid is also how gaps are found later (section 15).

Is the customer realistic? Read 30, fix, read 30 again

20 / 30

Round 1. The simulator contradicted its own situation ("I returned my stuff" for a cancelled order), made up amounts from Bitext's {{Refund Amount}} slots, put every detail in the first message, and thanked support three or four times instead of ending.

27 / 30

Round 2 (bar: 25). Goals name the returned items; the knowledge says what happened; Bitext slots are filled with neutral words; rules forbid invented amounts, dates and faults, and say to end once the goal is met.

How I'd say it in an interview"A fake customer that's too polite or too informed teaches the agent nothing. I set a bar before reading, 25 of 30 realistic, read the customer side only, and fixed the causes rather than the examples: what the customer knows, what it may not invent, when it stops. It went from 20 to 27."

8 · Fifteen checks

A strong teacher is still wrong sometimes, and a student copies mistakes as faithfully as strengths. So every conversation goes through every check, and one failure anywhere rejects it. Code checks run first: exact, free and repeatable. Two model judges cover what code cannot read. Each check has a hand-written conversation it must pass and one it must fail (tests/test_synth_verify.py).

CheckRejects a conversation when
schemaa tool call fails its argument model, or call markup reaches a reply
tool_before_factan order or tracking number is stated before any lookup returned it
grounded_numbersa number, day count or date is not in the results before it; "N days ago" arithmetic is refused outright
citationsa [kb:...] id was not returned by a search
end_statean eligible return gets no proposal, an ineligible one gets one, or a reply calls a return done
escalationa lost parcel is never handed off, an easy case is, or a hand-off or ticket is claimed without escalate_to_human
call_limita turn ran out of model calls and fell back to the fixed hand-off reply
privacya phone number, email, another customer's details, or Nova's instructions or tool names appear in a reply
right_orderthe customer named an item and Nova looked up a different order first
needless_toola tool is called for a greeting, "are you a bot?", or a plain thanks
lengtha reply over 80 words, or written as a list
stylea closing offer, several paragraphs, "I will ensure", a promised time after a hand-off, emails or links no result mentioned
dedupsame scenario, same calls and a near-identical opening as a conversation already kept
judge_tonea model judge fails it: ignores the customer, lectures, apologises twice, contradicts itself
judge_factsa model judge reading every reply against its results fails it: a misread result, a store rule no search gave, a worked-out weekday, a process nothing supports

Why this order

Code checks cost nothing and never disagree with themselves, so they run on everything and remove the obvious failures before the judges spend GPU time. The judges are reserved for what needs reading: whether a reply matches the meaning of a result, not just its numbers.

How I'd say it in an interview"Anything I could check in code, I checked in code: grounded numbers, citations, end states, privacy. Every check has a unit test with a conversation that must pass and one that must fail. The LLM judges only do what code can't: reading whether a reply actually says what the tool result says."

A judge is a model too, so it was tested before its verdicts were used: kept conversations with one reply rewritten to contain a planted fault, mixed with the untouched originals.

Hand reads of kept conversations

The bar: no more than 5 bad in 100 kept. First read: 17 bad of 90: worked-out day counts, hand-offs that never happened, invented processes ("send it back"), a made-up citation format. One cause was a bug worth remembering: the number check had passed "7 days" because a 7 appeared in every result, inside a time like 07:14. Day counts and dates are now matched whole. Second read: about 7 of 37, all misreadings only a reader with the results could see. That residue is exactly what the fact-check judge was built for.

10 · Keep rates and where they fall

The same 200 situations were run three times as the teacher's rules improved, with every check as it stood at the end. Better rules raised the keep rate from 46% to 57%. Generation is cheap compared with bad data, so the bar stayed where it was.

Keep rate on the same 200 situations. r4 added a three-call habit, citing only returned ids and "Nova cannot cancel"; r5 said what to do instead of "I cannot cancel" and told the judge what Nova sees.
First round. The fact-check judge was the strictest check: one slip anywhere in a conversation fails it.
Keep rate per scenario over all runs (1,267 of 3,761, 34%). Easy scenarios keep three in four; eligible returns keep one in sixteen.

The scenarios that keep least are the ones where the agent must do the most: decide a return, hand off a real problem, calm an angry customer. That is also where the 2B fails most on the golden set, so filtering hardest exactly where the data is thinnest is a real cost, not an accident. Section 15 is about recovering some of it.

11 · From conversations to training examples

Nova makes one model call at a time: each call either calls a tool or replies. So a conversation becomes one training example per model call, each holding everything before that call, rendered exactly as served, with the teacher's output as the only target.

A kept conversation (typo persona, split shipment), shortened. Five model calls, five examples: three tool calls and two replies. Tool results are input, never targets.

Salvaging the clean steps of rejected conversations

The fact judge rejects a whole conversation for one slip, but training is per step. When the judge names the first bad reply, every step before it is clean by the judge's own reading, and the code checks already passed the whole conversation. Those leading steps were kept as training examples: 4,772 in the first mix. Steps at and after the slip are dropped, so no example's target is a reply the judge failed.

The loss sees only the target

Every example is rendered with the chat template and the six tool schemas. Only the target's tokens count in the loss; the system prompt, the customer, earlier calls and every tool result are masked. The hard case is a step after two tool calls in the same turn: the template marks three assistant spans, and only the last may train. tests/test_masking.py checks exactly that case, and every training run checks the mask on 200 rows before step 1.

12 · Training SFT-2a and SFT-2b

Two LoRA adapters (r 16, alpha 16, the seven attention and MLP projections, 10.9M trainable parameters), one epoch of 593 steps of 16 examples, cosine schedule, AdamW 8-bit, bf16. Both on one Modal A100-80GB, about 15 minutes each, 43.8 GB peak. One question separates them: does starting from SFT-1's voice help?

SFT-2a

From the base model, learning rate 2e-4. Adapter sft2a-r16-lr2e-4-traj_v1.

SFT-2b

Continued from SFT-1, learning rate 1e-4 (half, to keep more of the voice). Adapter sft2b-r16-lr1e-4-traj_v1.

Validation loss on 266 examples from whole held-out conversations (v2: 359, a different set, so not directly comparable). Step 0 is the starting model: LoRA's B matrix starts at zero.

SFT-1 starts much further from this data (0.878 against 0.530): what it learned from Bitext (offer to check, ask permission, never call) is the opposite of these targets. It is unlearned within 100 steps, and from there the curves run together. Starting from SFT-1 bought nothing measurable.

Why SFT-2a and not SFT-2b

69 against 67 is a tie at this size (eight cases only 2a gets right, six only 2b). What decided it was returns, 7 against 3: in three return cases SFT-2b proposed the return straight after a help-center search, without looking at the order, and once called the proposal "created". Returns are the one flow that writes to the database. SFT-2a also stands on its own, with no dependency on SFT-1.

Biderman et al. (2024), "LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less", predicts SFT-2b keeps more of SFT-1's voice. It does, barely: "Would you like…" in 15 of 111 replies against 13 for SFT-2a, and 89 for SFT-1. With targets this different, the second stage overwrote almost all of the first.

How I'd say it in an interview"Two adapters, one question: does starting from the voice model help? It didn't. SFT-1 started further from the data and spent its first hundred steps unlearning. The two tied overall, so I chose on the flow that matters most: returns write to the database, and SFT-2a looks the order up before proposing, where SFT-2b sometimes didn't."

14 · What SFT-2a still gets wrong

All 38 failed cases were read one by one and tagged with their main failure.

Examples: pol-022 says the help center "does not cover" a 15-day window it does state; esc-001 tells a customer whose parcel has not moved for a week that "it has not been lost"; una-004 declines gift wrap without searching or offering a teammate.

Three of these trace back to the data, not the model, which is the useful part:

  • Hand-offs for delays. The teacher was told a late parcel is not a lost one, and the filters rejected late parcels offered a hand-off. The student learned it too well: "not lost" even after a week without movement.
  • Out of scope. The no-tool chats taught a short, polite decline; the golden set wants a search and an offered teammate.
  • The fallback reply. Four calls in a turn still end on the fixed hand-off line.

15 · The second round: one word, 454 conversations

The plan was 2,000 kept conversations with no empty cell in the coverage grid. The first round kept 1,006 and left 28 of 128 cells empty, all in the hardest scenarios. Grouping the fact judge's reasons by scenario, rather than counting them overall, found the main cause: in 454 conversations its only objection was a hand-off reply promising a time ("a teammate will reply to you shortly"). The hand-off tool's own result says to tell the customer "without promising a time", and the judge sided with it. Hand-offs cluster in exactly the thin scenarios (lost and delayed parcels, rejected returns, frustrated customers), so one habit emptied whole rows of the grid.

Repair

synth/repair.py removes the time words in code, only in sentences about a teammate acting. The 725 repaired conversations that had failed for it were judged again: 327 passed.

Prevent

The style check now rejects a time promise after a hand-off, the generator sends such a reply back for a rewrite, and teacher rules r6 forbid it.

Fill

Why it was missed the first timeThe judge's rubric had been changed to accept "shortly" after the planted test, and the effect of that change was never measured again. 16 · A coding agent as the teacher

By the fill runs the GPU credit was gone, and new cloud GPU quota could not be granted. The generator only needs something that speaks the OpenAI chat API, so synth/agent_server.py became a mailbox: it answers like a model server, but writes each request to a queue folder and waits for an answer file. A coding agent reads the request (who to play, the instructions, the conversation and every tool result, each tool's arguments and allowed values) and answers it. Everything else is unchanged: the real tools run every call, the student's view is recorded, and every check applies.

Several agents can work in parallel: claiming a request renames its file, which is atomic, so no request is answered twice. The instructions are shown in full once, then only the lines that changed.
  • Risk kept small on purpose. A provider's terms may limit training on its output, and heavy automated use can get an account restricted. So the run was small (two or three agents), adversarial customers stayed in a separate plan for the open-weights teacher, and the instruction was to stop at the first warning or rate limit. The agent and model are recorded as the teacher in every conversation.
  • Judged by hand. 12 conversations; 7 passed the code checks; those 7 were read against the same tone and fact rubrics. 3 were kept: two failed on tone, and two told the customer "your return is all set" for a return only proposed. verify.py now catches "all set" and "sorted" too.
How I'd say it in an interview"When I ran out of GPU credit, I didn't change the pipeline, I swapped the teacher behind the same API. The mailbox looks like a model server to the generator, so the tools, the recording and all fifteen checks stayed exactly the same. The volume was tiny and every conversation was read by hand, and that reading even found a new failure pattern."

17 · v2 against v1, and the release gate

SFT-2a was trained again on the v2 mix (10,446 examples from 1,267 kept conversations) with the same settings. It ran on one L4 on a Google Compute Engine VM at batch 4 × 4 with gradient checkpointing, because no A100 was available: 653 steps in 84 minutes, 5.9 GB peak. Validation loss fell to 0.217. Then both adapters ran on one vLLM server on that L4, each on its own database copy: the golden set three times each, and 50 fresh cases written after the golden set.

v1 scored 69/107 on the L4, exactly as on Modal, so the change of serving hardware moved nothing.

v2 is not better. Every gap is inside the 95% intervals, but golden and fresh point the same way. Of the five golden cases whose result changed in two runs or more, four moved against v2. In most of v2's extra misses it takes the right steps and leaves a fact out of the reply: a hand-off without the delivery date, a return proposal without the refund amount. v2's replies after a hand-off are shorter (25 words on average against 29). A likely cause, not tested: the repair removed the time words and left terser hand-off sentences for the student to copy.

20 · Defending the design: interview questions

Why synthetic data instead of real support logs?

The store is fictional, so there are none, and real logs would need consent and redaction anyway. Generating inside the real environment gives conversations whose facts are all true for that environment, with full control over coverage.

Why not have a big model write whole conversations?

It would invent the store: orders, statuses and tracking numbers that don't exist. With the teacher acting inside the loop, it only chooses actions; the database and help center supply every fact, and the checks prove it.

Why a 27B teacher and not the strongest model available?

The strongest open model didn't fit four H100s and eight would have doubled the budget; the frontier API was blocked and its terms may not allow training on its output. The 27B is Apache-2.0, fits one A100 at full precision, and shares the student's chat format.

How does the student learn the teacher's rules without seeing them?

Context distillation: the teacher's prompt has 28 extra rules, but only Nova's serving prompt is recorded as the input. The targets carry the behaviour the rules produced. Rules the teacher ignored were enforced in code instead.

How did you know the simulated customers were realistic?

A bar set before reading (25 of 30), a read of the customer side only, and fixes to causes, not examples: what the customer knows, what it may not invent, when it stops. 20/30 became 27/30.

A 34% keep rate: isn't that wasteful?

Generation costs cents per conversation; training on a wrong fact costs the model. The student copies mistakes as faithfully as strengths, so the bar stays high. Better teacher rules raised the rate instead (46% → 57% on the same situations).

How do you trust an LLM judge?

By testing it like any classifier before using it: planted faults mixed with originals. Tone 48/48; facts caught 36/36 planted faults, and its "false alarms" on originals were mostly real slips. Hand reads then checked what was kept.

Isn't salvaging steps from rejected conversations risky?

Only the steps before the first reply the judge names are kept, and the code checks passed the whole conversation. No kept example's target is a reply that failed a check.

Why one example per model call?

That is how Nova runs: one call, then a tool runs or a reply goes out. Each example is the exact input of a serving call with one target, so training and serving see the same thing. The loss covers only that target, which the masking test proves on two-call steps.

Why choose SFT-2a when SFT-2b was within two cases?

A two-case gap is noise, so I chose on the flow with the highest stakes: returns write to the database, and SFT-2a looked orders up before proposing (7/15 against 3/15). It also doesn't depend on SFT-1.

Is 69 of 107 good?

It is 64%, from 27% for the same model without fine-tuning. The frontier model used as the ceiling scored 64% on the 83 golden cases it finished in the same loop, with the same four-call limit (04_comparison.md), at about 50 times the serving cost. The remaining failures are read, counted and mostly traced to the data.

Why did more data not help the second time?

The extra data was more of the same distribution, and the repair may have made hand-off replies terser. The errors left are specific, such as a help-center section misread or a fact left out, and need data aimed at them, or preference pairs from the rejects.

You missed the 2,000-conversation target. Why ship?

Because the measured result is what ships, not the data count: 69/107 against 29, with no format errors. The second round then showed that closing most of the gap (26 of 28 empty cells) didn't move the score, so the target itself was less important than it looked.

Why not generate everything with the coding agent?

Terms and scale: a provider's terms may limit training on its output, and heavy automated use risks the account. It was a small, hand-judged stopgap with the agent recorded as the teacher; the open-weights teacher remains the main source.

How did you keep cost under control?

Pilot first, then a measured budget; a dollar cap checked every 30 seconds, a stall stop, and after one overrun a hard wall-clock limit. The whole stage fit in free credit.

What would you do differently?

Group the judges' rejection reasons by scenario from the first run, re-measure after every rubric change, and check every sign-off item with its evidence before calling the stage done.

21 · Limits, what comes next, glossary

Known limits

  • 107 golden cases and 50 fresh: differences under about 8 points are noise; the v1/v2 comparison is directional only.
  • One teacher family: its blind spots (for example the "shortly" habit) pass to the student unless a check catches them.
  • Hand reads by one reader; 3 agent-written conversations judged by hand rather than by the model judges.
  • 2 coverage cells empty and the adversarial cells left for the open-weights teacher.

What comes next

  • Hand-offs that carry the facts: the delivery date or refund amount next to the ticket number, and after any wording repair, a check of what the replies still say.
  • Preference tuning on the rejects: nova-rejects-v2 keeps every rejected conversation with its reasons, the raw material for chosen/rejected pairs.
  • A guardrail layer in serving: strip tool names from replies in code, the main hard-rule hit for both adapters.
