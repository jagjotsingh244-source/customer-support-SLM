1 · What SFT-1 was for, and the result

The baseline (Part 3) showed the prompted Qwen3.5-2B gets 25 of 100 golden cases right, against 58 for Gemini on the same loop. Part 4 built a dataset to teach how Nova sounds: 4,447 training rows of real customer phrasing with replies rewritten into Nova's voice and every invented fact removed. SFT-1 is the first fine-tune on it. It was always meant to teach tone, not tools; the question was what else it would change.

2 · The memory wall: why LoRA

Training needs far more memory than running. A full fine-tune with the Adam optimizer in mixed precision keeps, for every parameter:

About 16 bytes per trained parameter. The optimizer state (12 of the 16) is the part that grows with what you train.

For Qwen3.5-2B's 1.88 billion text parameters that is 30.1 GB before a single activation (35.4 GB counting the vision tower that loads with it). A free Kaggle T4 has 15 GB. Full fine-tuning is out by a factor of two.

LoRA (low-rank adaptation) freezes all of those weights and trains a small add-on instead: 0.49% of the parameters at rank 16. The frozen weights still cost 2 bytes each in 16-bit, but they have no gradients and no optimizer state, so what remains is mostly the model itself.

Training memory. The full fine-tune bars are weights, gradients and optimizer only; the LoRA bars are a whole training step, measured with max_memory_allocated.

Why 16-bit LoRA and not 4-bit QLoRA

QLoRA stores the frozen weights in 4 bits to fit bigger models on small GPUs. It was not needed and not advised: Unsloth recommends against quantized training for Qwen3.5, and a 16-bit LoRA on a 2B model already fits a T4 with 10 GB to spare. Quantizing would add error to every forward pass to save memory that was not short.

How I'd say it in an interview"Full fine-tuning costs about 16 bytes per parameter with Adam: 30 GB for this model, twice a T4. LoRA trains half a percent of the parameters, so the optimizer state nearly disappears and the bill is basically the frozen weights: 4.9 GB measured. I didn't use QLoRA because it wasn't needed and Unsloth advises against it for Qwen3.5."

3 · LoRA in one picture

For each adapted weight matrix W (shape d_out × d_in), LoRA learns two thin matrices: B (d_out × r) and A (r × d_in). Their product B·A has the same shape as W but rank at most r, so it can only change the layer in r directions. The layer computes W·x + (α/r)·B·A·x.

One MLP projection (down_proj) at r = 16. The trained part is 1% of the frozen matrix it sits beside.
  • B starts at zero, so at step 0 the fine-tuned model is identical to the base. That makes step 0's evaluation the base model's score, a free baseline on every curve in this guide.
  • α / r scales the update. SFT-1 uses α = r = 16 (scale 1), Unsloth's recipe for Qwen3.5. The ablation (section 8) keeps α = r for the same reason: so rank changes capacity without also changing the step size, at least nominally.
  • It can be merged or served beside the base. Merging folds B·A into W (no extra cost at inference); serving it as an add-on lets one base model in vLLM serve several adapters. Nova serves it as an add-on, and section 13 checks both forms give the same answers.

4 · Reading the model before choosing targets

Which matrices get an adapter is the first real decision. The usual recipe lists seven names (q/k/v/o_proj, gate/up/down_proj). Before using it, training/lora_budget.py built the model from its config with no weights (on the "meta" device, so a laptop CPU can inspect a 2B model in seconds), printed every linear layer, and counted the adapter by hand.

Qwen3.5-2B is a hybrid: three linear-attention layers, then one full-attention layer, six times over. The usual target names only exist in the six full-attention layers.

Three findings changed what the numbers meant:

  1. 18 of the 24 layers have no q_proj. They use linear attention (Gated DeltaNet) with different projection names, so the standard recipe adapts attention in only 6 layers. The MLP, which every layer has, carries 86% of the adapter.
  2. q_proj is 4,096 wide, not 2,048, because it also produces the attention output gate (8 heads × 256 × 2). A hand count that assumed a square projection would be short by 196,608 parameters at r = 16.
  3. A 331M-parameter vision tower loads with the model. target_modules="all-linear", a popular shortcut, would quietly wrap 98 of its layers and train them on text.
Trainable parameters per target at r = 16: 10,911,744 in total, 681,984 per unit of rank. Blue: MLP projections in all 24 layers; orange: attention projections in the 6 full-attention layers.

7 · The first run

278 steps on one T4 in 40 minutes (41 for the whole run, including 12 evaluations with probes).

Training loss (light), validation loss and held-out-intent loss. Neither evaluation loss turns up, so there is no overfitting in one epoch; held-out loss is flat from step 225.

The gap between validation (0.621) and held-out intents (0.828) is the part that is specific to the trained intents; the rest transferred. Because nothing overfit, the final step was kept rather than an earlier checkpoint.

8 · The rank ablation, and how not to misread it

How much adapter capacity does the voice need? Three short runs changed only the rank: r = 8, 16, 64 on the same seeded 1,000 rows, one epoch (63 steps), evaluation every 10 steps. Alpha moved with r (8/8, 16/16, 64/64): holding alpha at 16 would scale an r = 64 update by 0.25 and an r = 8 update by 2, adding a second difference.

Validation loss by rank. The grey dashed line is the full-data r = 16 run over its first 63 steps, for scale.

The obvious reading, and why it is wrong

r = 64 has the lowest loss everywhere, held-out intents included. But its lead shrinks: 0.46 over r = 8 at step 10, 0.12 at the end. A larger rank with α = r also takes bigger steps (a rank-64 update is a sum of 64 rank-one pieces, each moved by Adam at the same rate), so it learns faster. A real capacity limit would look the other way round: the small adapter stalling late and the gap opening up. No curve stalls.

  • More data beat more rank: r = 16 on all rows ends 0.11 below r = 64 on 1,000 rows.
  • The probes were the same in kind at all three ranks, with the same faults. r = 64's "hello" reply was the one clearly worse answer.
  • r = 64 costs more: a 4× larger adapter (175 MB against 44 MB in fp32), a higher --max-lora-rank in vLLM, a slightly wider seen/unseen gap.

Choice: r = 16, unchanged, which also meant the full run already trained was the final model. The open question is written down: holding the step size fixed across ranks (rsLoRA's α/√r scaling) on the full data would separate capacity from speed.

How I'd say it in an interview"The rank-64 run had the best loss, but its lead shrank from 0.46 to 0.12 over the run. With alpha tied to rank, a bigger rank also means a bigger effective step, so a 63-step run rewards speed, not capacity. Compare the curves, not the last point."

9 · Evaluating a fine-tune: four checks

Fine-tuning moves a model toward its data in every direction the data points, including directions nobody intended. The unwanted part is called forgetting, and it only shows outside the training distribution. So SFT-1 gets four checks, each answering a different question, plus a check that the served model is the trained one.

Checks 1 and 2 look inside the training distribution; checks 3 and 4 look outside it, where the cost of fine-tuning shows.

All four compare like with like: the same prompts, greedy decoding or the baseline's exact settings, and the base model run through the same path. The golden run used the same machine and database copy as the baseline floor run, so the only difference is the adapter.

12 · Why it happened: the data's habits

Counting the same phrases in the 4,447 training replies and in SFT-1's 100 golden replies:

Share of replies with each habit. The model reproduced the data's habits almost exactly, and turned the most common one up.

The training rows used the serving system prompt word for word, including its fallback rule: "If you have neither [tool results], say you will check or hand off." But the rows had no tool schemas and no tool calls. So 4,447 times, the model learned that the reply to a customer under this prompt is a promise to check, followed by a question. At serving time the tool schemas are there, and it still writes the reply it was trained on. It learned to obey one rule of its prompt (say you will check) at the expense of the others (call a tool before saying anything about an order).

A decision reversed by the evidencePart 4 argued for leaving the tool schemas out of the training prompt: tools listed in every example and never used would teach the model to ignore them. The result shows the model learned to ignore them anyway. With tool-free data alone there is no right answer; the fix is the data mix. In SFT-2 every row carries the schemas, and the rows that need a tool call one, so the model learns when to call, not whether tools exist.
How I'd say it in an interview"I read all 18 cases it newly failed. Every one was the same habit: offer, ask permission, don't act. Then I counted the habit in the training data: 83% of replies. The model didn't break; it learned the data. The fix is in the data, not the training settings."

13 · The export check

Nova is trained and evaluated in Transformers but served by vLLM, with the adapter loaded as a LoRA module. If the two disagree in kind (one reply runs to the token cap while the other stops), the cause is almost always the chat template or the end-of-turn token, and every serving result after it would be wrong.

The export check runs on a Kaggle T4 x2: vLLM on one GPU, Transformers on the other.

Identical text on two stacks is unusual enough to check before believing. The files differ in one field: the completion token count is one higher in vLLM on every reply, because vLLM counts the end-of-turn token and the local client does not. So both stop on the same token, which is exactly what the check is for. The vLLM log shows the adapter loaded, and its replies are SFT-1's, not the base model's.

Scope: this tested the adapter served as an add-on (how Nova is served) against the adapter merged in Transformers. A merged 16-bit copy served by vLLM was not tested; it matters only if deployment switches to a merged model.

14 · What changes for SFT-2

SFT-1 is not a failed run: it shows a voice can be taught in 40 minutes on a free GPU, and it measures exactly what tool-free data costs. SFT-2 keeps the method and changes the data.

#ChangeEvidence it answers
1Mix in conversations that use the tools: multi-turn traces in the serving format with tool schemas, tool calls and tool results; loss on Nova's calls and replies, not on tool results. Cover search vs ask, stopping once the facts are in, the return flow, and real escalate_to_human calls.Zero tool calls in 100 golden cases; 16 of 18 new failures offer to look up instead of looking up.
2Rewrite the replies' habits before reusing them. No "Would you like me to check…" on order and policy questions; real hand-offs where the customer asks for a person; no "I don't have access" (false for Nova); shorter, about 20 words, leading with the answer or action. 83% of training replies ask permission; 601 claim no access; the blind reader preferred short and direct 23 to 2.
3Train every row with the full serving prompt, tool schemas included, and watch tool use during training: a few golden-style probes with tools at every evaluation step.The collapse was visible in the probes at step 25 but nothing measured tool use until after the run.

Targets for SFT-2: required calls back to at least the floor's 54/100, all required above 25/100, the style metrics held, and a blind read that prefers it to the base model.

16 · Defending the design: interview questions

Why LoRA instead of full fine-tuning?

Full fine-tuning needs about 16 bytes per parameter with Adam: 30 GB for this model, twice a T4. LoRA trains 0.49% of the parameters, so the optimizer state almost disappears: 4.94 GB measured. For teaching tone on 4,447 rows, a low-rank update is also enough; the ablation found no capacity limit even at r = 8.

Why not QLoRA?

It saves memory I did not need and adds quantization error to every forward pass. Unsloth advises against quantized training for Qwen3.5.

How did you choose which layers to adapt?

I printed the model first. It is a hybrid: 18 of 24 layers use linear attention with different names, so the usual seven targets adapt attention in only 6 layers, and all-linear would train the vision tower. I kept the seven (MLPs in every layer carry 86% of the adapter) and wrote the linear-attention gap down as a known choice to revisit.

How do you know your parameter count is right?

I counted r × (d_in + d_out) for every wrapped layer by hand from the real shapes, compared with PEFT at three ranks, and with the training run. All matched exactly: 10,911,744 at r = 16.

Why rank 16 when rank 64 had lower loss?

Its lead shrank from 0.46 to 0.12 over the run, the signature of a bigger effective step, not of missing capacity. More data mattered more (full-data r = 16 beat it), the replies were the same in kind, and r = 64 is 4× larger.

Why keep alpha equal to r in the ablation?

The update is scaled by α/r. Fixed α = 16 would scale r = 64 by 0.25 and r = 8 by 2, so the runs would differ in two ways. Even with α = r the step size still differs in effect, which is why the result is read from the curves.

Why evaluate a tone fine-tune with tools?

Because Nova always runs with tools, and forgetting only shows outside the training distribution. Without check 4, SFT-1 would have looked like a clean win.

Isn't losing tool use a failed experiment?

No. It is the measurement that justifies the next dataset's mix, with a number on it: 54 → 11 cases with every required call. It also showed what did work: the voice can be taught in 40 minutes, and nothing was invented.

Why a blind read when you have style metrics?

The metrics check rules I wrote. The blind read checks whether the rules were right, and they were not: the reader preferred the shorter, more direct base replies 23 to 2.

How do you know vLLM serves the same model you evaluated?

Ten fixed prompts, greedy, through vLLM with the adapter as a LoRA module and through Transformers with it merged: identical text on all ten, with the base model as a control. The only difference was a token count off by one, explained by how each counts the end-of-turn token.

Why did the golden run happen on a laptop?

The baseline floor ran there with the same database copy, so SFT-1 on the same machine differs only by the adapter. The export check showed the served model gives the same text, so serving would not change the result.

How would you fix the tool-use loss?

In the data: tool-using multi-turn conversations mixed in, the permission habit and the false "no access" line rewritten out, tool schemas in every row, and tool-use probes during training so a collapse shows at step 25.

Why not just train fewer steps to forget less?

Tool calls went to zero, not down, and the habit was already set by step 25. Fewer steps would keep less of the voice without bringing tools back. It is still a cheap experiment worth one run, to see how fast the habit sets in.

What would you do differently?

Run the blind read on the training replies before training, and add tool-calling probes to the training loop from the first run.

17 · Limits, what comes next, glossary

Known limits

  • One reader, 30 prompts in the blind check, and no tools in it; the direction is clear, the exact share is not.
  • 100 golden cases: one case is 4 points in a 25-case category; differences under about 8 points are noise.
  • The ablation ran 63 steps on 1,000 rows; capacity and step size are not separated.
  • The linear-attention layers carry no adapter; whether that limits behaviour change is untested.

What comes next

  • Retrieval over the help center, so policy facts come from documents instead of memory.
  • SFT-2: the three changes in section 14, judged by the same four checks and the export check.

Alpha (α)A scale on the add-on, applied as α / r.
MergingAdding B·A into W after training, so the add-on costs nothing at inference.
ForgettingA fine-tune losing an ability its training data never showed.
Golden set100 hand-written cases with expected tool calls and facts, scored the same way every run.
Export checkConfirming the serving stack produces the same answers as the training stack.
