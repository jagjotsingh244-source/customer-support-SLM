1 · What this data is for, and what it is not

The baseline showed the untrained 2B model gets 25 of 100 cases right. Part of what it gets wrong is behaviour: rambling, asking the wrong question, not ending with a clear next step. The first fine-tune (SFT-1) targets that: how Nova sounds. What Nova does with tools comes in a later fine-tune, trained on multi-turn conversations with real tool calls.

The source is Bitext, a public customer-support dataset: 26,872 question-and-answer pairs, about 1,000 for each of 27 intents in 11 categories, licensed CDLA-Sharing 1.0. Each row has the customer's message (instruction), an example reply (response), the intent, the category, and flags describing how the message is phrased.

Good forNot good for
Tone: polite, patient, structured repliesFacts: its replies describe procedures, menus and timelines of a company that does not exist
Realistic phrasing: typos, slang, keywords, rudeness, each taggedTool use: no reply ever calls a tool
Coverage: ordering, refunds, delivery, payments, accountsMulti-turn: every example is one question and one answer

That table decides the whole approach. Every Bitext reply carries two things: a form (acknowledge, respond to what was asked, offer a next step) and content (invented procedures). The fine-tune takes the form and none of the content, because in this project facts come only from the help center and the tools (the rule from Part 1: "if it changes often, retrieve it; if it belongs to one customer, fetch it").

The rewritten reply keeps acknowledging, responding and offering a step, and drops everything the model would otherwise learn to invent.
How I'd say it in an interview"Bitext is good for tone and realistic customer phrasing, and bad for facts: every reply describes procedures for a company that doesn't exist. So I kept the form and removed the content. The first fine-tune learns how Nova sounds; facts come from retrieval and tools at answer time."

2 · Looking before training: five questions

A model is a compressed copy of its training data, habits included. The data was measured before any decision was made (data_prep/bitext_eda.ipynb, all 26,872 rows, lengths counted with Qwen3.5-2B's own tokenizer).

Balance: the counts lie

Every intent has 950 to 1,000 rows, so a bar chart of rows looks perfectly balanced. Counting distinct questions tells a different story: 59% of cancel_order rows repeat an earlier question. A model trained on the raw rows sees cancellation about twice as often as its real share, and drifts toward it.

Rows per intent (grey) against distinct questions after dedup (normalised message or first 12 words). No question appears under two intents.

Length: replies are essays

The median reply is 104 tokens (90 words), the 99th percentile 366. Only 23% of replies fall in Nova's 25–70 word target; 76% are longer. Length also varies 7× by intent (median 48 tokens for check_cancellation_fee, 340 for check_refund_policy), which would teach the model that some topics deserve an essay. Lengths are in tokens because the training limits are in tokens: Qwen's tokenizer uses 1.21 tokens per English word here.

Reply length in words: Bitext (top) against the final rewritten replies (bottom). The green band is the 25–70 word target.

Placeholders: slots the model learns to fill

13,006 replies (48%) contain a {{...}} placeholder, 391 kinds in all; a cancel_order reply has 8 on average, which is where the invented menu paths live. 6,670 customer messages contain one too ({{Order Number}}, {{Person Name}}...).

The ten most common placeholders in replies. Each is a fact the model would learn to produce from nothing.

4 · The pipeline end to end

One script, data_prep/prepare_bitext.py, does steps 1 to 6; review_rewrites.py adds the meaning check and the hand review; build_chat_dataset.py formats and splits. Every row keeps its Bitext row number, intent, flags and the original message and reply, so any training example can be traced back to its source.

Rows surviving each step. Sampling is the biggest cut by design; each check after it removes only what fails.

Reproducible

Every random choice is seeded (3407), per row where it matters, so the same row gets the same fake order number every run.

Resumable

Every model answer is cached by row number and prompt version as it arrives. A run stopped halfway lost nothing.

Traceable

Reports per intent and per flag are written on every run: data_prep/reports/bitext_rewrite_report.md.

5 · Placeholders: the customer's, never Nova's

The obvious fix for {{Online Order Interaction}} is to fill it with a NimbusCart value: "Go to My Account › Order History › Cancel." That makes it worse. NimbusCart's site has no such menu, and the real rule (orders cannot be cancelled once shipped) lives in the help center. Filling the blank turns a generic invented procedure into a specific, convincing invented procedure.

In Nova's replies: never fill

The rewrite removes the sentence's need for the fact. Any rewritten reply that still contains {{ is dropped automatically.

In customer messages: fill with fake values

These are the customer's own details; real customers type real order numbers. Filled with seeded values in NimbusCart's formats.

Placeholder in the messageFilled withExample
{{Order Number}} (2,907)5-digit IDs from 10001, the store's format"cancel order 13569"
{{Person Name}} (887)Indian names (Faker, en_IN)"invoices from Kamya Baral"
{{Refund Amount}}, {{Currency Symbol}}rupee amounts"a rebate of ₹1,299"
{{Delivery Country}}40% India, 60% other countries"do ya ship to Nepal"
{{Account Type}}, {{Account Category}}nothing: the row is droppedNimbusCart has no account tiers

Two details only reading the rows found. 239 refund messages say "{{Refund Amount}} dollars" in a store that sells in rupees; the word is replaced along with the amount. And "do you ship to {{Delivery Country}}" is a question about shipping abroad; filling it with "India" every time would make it meaningless, so most get a foreign country. The help center says nothing about shipping abroad, so those replies must not answer it either: they offer to check or to connect a teammate.

The untouched messages, all 26,872 with placeholders intact, are saved separately as nimbus-bitext-questions: they become the opening lines of the tool-using conversations in a later fine-tune.

How I'd say it in an interview"Filling Bitext's blanks with my store's values would have made the model more convincing and more wrong: it would learn a specific menu path my site doesn't have. So reply-side blanks are never filled; the rewrite removes the need for the fact. Customer-side blanks are the customer's own details, so those I fill with seeded fake values in my store's formats."

6 · Sampling that protects rare phrasings

From the 22,576 usable rows, the sample takes up to 300 per kept intent and 100 per redirect intent: 5,500 rows. A plain random sample has a problem. Dedup already thinned keyword-only messages (short messages repeat more), and random sampling thinned them again: 8.3% of raw rows, 4.7% of a plain sample. So rows with a rare flag (keyword, negation, offensive, politeness) get double weight.

Share of rows with each rare flag. With double weight (blue), every rare phrasing is at or above its share of the deduplicated pool.

Weighted sampling without replacement uses the Efraimidis–Spirakis method: each row gets the key u1/w for a seeded random u and weight w, and the top keys per intent are kept. (pandas refused weights without replacement; implementing it took four lines.)

7 · The rewrite, and the rewriter's habits

A larger model rewrites every sampled reply into Nova's voice. This is a small case of data distillation: a big model reshapes data for a small one. The model was gemini-4-hydrogen at thinking level "low", with requests running 16 at a time, never one by one.

Rewrite this customer-support reply for Nova, NimbusCart's assistant.
- 25 to 70 words. Warm, plain, no exclamation marks, no lists, no headers.
- Remove every procedure, menu path, URL, timeline, number and {{placeholder}}.
  Do not replace them with new details.
- Do not say anything has been done, and do not promise an outcome.
- Do not explain when something is or is not possible: that comes from the policy or the order.
- Respond to the customer's actual message, including any frustration.
- End with exactly one next step Nova can take: check their order, look up the policy,
  ask one clarifying question, or offer a teammate.          (+ rules added from each round below)

The rewriter passed the automatic checks 99.9% of the time on its first full run, and the output was still not usable. The pass rate measured form. What it could not see were the rewriter's own habits, and a 2B model trained on its output would copy them exactly. They were found by one simple method: after every run, count the most common openings, closings and phrases.

RoundFound by countingFix
v1 (50 rows)Stiff ("I do not", "I am"), filler ("I am here to help you")Use contractions; no filler sentences
v2 (50 rows)19% opened "You're looking to"; invented rules ("not always possible once an order is placed", while NimbusCart allows changes until shipping)Ban stock openers; never explain when something is possible
v3 (5,500)31% of replies said "I'd be glad"v4: ban "I'd be glad / happy"

Only rows that showed a habit were redone; the others kept their earlier rewrite, because the later prompts only added bans. A rewrite counts as valid under a newer prompt version if it does not show any habit that version bans.

Customercould ya help me checking the current status of my refund

v3–v4Waiting on a refund can be frustrating, and I can see why you'd want an update. I don't have your order details in front of me yet, so I'm not able to see the current status from here. Would you like me to check your order for you?

Two habits in one reply: a feeling the customer never showed, and filler that contradicts the offer that follows.

In the final 5,276 replies: none says "I'd be glad / happy", none names a feeling to a calm customer, none has the "can't see" filler, none makes a store claim; the median is 34 words and no opening is used by more than 3.5% of replies.

How I'd say it in an interview"Distillation copies the teacher's habits along with its quality. The first full run passed 99.9% of my format checks and a third of replies said 'I'd be glad'. So after every run I counted the most common phrases, turned each habit into a rule, and redid only the rows that had it. A pass rate tells you about the checks, not about the data."

8 · Checking meaning: rules, a judge, a human

Three layers, each catching what the one before cannot.

Testing the judge before trusting it

A judge model is another model that can be wrong. Before it saw any real data it was given 20 hand-written pairs: 8 good, and 4 planted mistakes of each kind (a reply about a refund when the customer asked about cancelling; "you can cancel within seven days from the Orders page"; a reply offering three next steps). First result 18/20. The two misses taught two things:

  • It accepted a reply offering a choice of three next steps. The prompt now says "exactly one, and a choice of several is false".
  • Two of my own "good" examples were not: one stated a refund rule, one offered two next steps. The judge was stricter than me, and right.

After the fixes it judged all 20 correctly, twice in a row. The test stays in the repo (data_prep/check_judge.py) for any judge change.

What the judge caught, and what only a human found

The judge stops clear invented facts and misses vague ones. Vague quantity claims became a pattern check (108 rows dropped).

Reading 200 pairs by hand was the step that measured the judge. Of the six rows rejected by hand (PayPal and Apple Pay listed as payment methods, "we don't have a toll-free number", two shipping-option claims, and "I can't connect you directly", when Nova can hand off), the judge caught three. Without the hand review, the judge's 98.9% acceptance rate would have looked like proof of quality.

How I'd say it in an interview"I didn't trust the LLM judge until I'd tested it: 20 pairs with planted mistakes first, then a hand review of 200 to measure what it misses. It caught every concrete invented fact but only half the vague ones, so I added a rule for those. The human review's job was to check the judge, not to replace it."

9 · Chat format and the loss mask

Three rules

  1. Train in the model's own template. Each row is stored as messages and rendered with tokenizer.apply_chat_template, the same call the server uses. A hand-built "Customer: ... Nova: ..." string would train a prompt shape the model never sees when serving.
  2. Learn only from Nova's tokens. Every token goes into the model, but the loss counts only the reply: labels of every other token are -100, which the loss ignores. Otherwise the model also learns to write the customer's side.
  3. No tool schemas in the system prompt. The prompt is v0, the serving text, with today's date and the customer's name seeded per row. Bitext replies never call a tool; showing tools in every example while never using them would teach "tools are present, ignore them".

Where the loss starts: the finding that mattered

The usual recipe starts training after <|im_start|>assistant\n. Rendering an example showed why that is wrong for this model: Qwen3.5's template puts an empty think block in every assistant turn, and the serving prompt already ends with that block. Training from the usual marker would teach Nova to write a block it never has to write.

<|im_start|>system\n
You are Nova, the support assistant for NimbusCart, an online store in India.\n
Today is Thursday 17 September 2026. The signed-in customer is Dipta Vig.\n
\n
Rules: [...]<|im_end|>\n
<|im_start|>user\n
I bought the same item twice, cancel order 12417<|im_end|>\n
<|im_start|>assistant\n
<think>\n
\n
</think>\n
\n
It's easy to accidentally buy the same item twice, and we can look into that duplicate order for you. Would you like me to check your order details?<|im_end|>\n
One training example as the model sees it (220 tokens; system rules shortened here). Grey: label -100, not trained. Green: the 33 trained tokens, Nova's reply and <|im_end|> so it learns to stop.

A second finding: in a longer conversation the template drops the think block from earlier assistant turns, exactly as it does for history when serving. So only the last assistant turn has the shape the model actually generates, and only that turn is trained; earlier turns are context. This matters for the multi-turn data later.

Proving it

training/masking.py computes the labels from character offsets; tests/test_masking.py (6 tests, in CI on every push) fails if any system or customer token is trained, if the serving prompt does not end with the marker, or if Unsloth's token-id search would not find the marker. That last failure is silent otherwise: every label becomes -100, the loss shows 0 or nan, and nothing is learned. After building the dataset, every one of the 5,276 examples was checked: its trained tokens decode to exactly its reply plus <|im_end|>.

4,447

test_unseen_intents: change_shipping_address and payment_issue, never trained on

Two rules. Split after dedup: otherwise near-copies sit on both sides and inflate validation scores. Hold out whole intents: a random 95/5 split only says whether the model fits data like its training data; two intents it has never seen say whether it learned support behaviour or memorised templates.

Sequence length from measurement

Every rendered example fits in 256 tokens, so nothing is truncated. About 172 of each example's tokens are the system prompt; the trained reply is a median 40.

max_seq_length is the 99th percentile (245) rounded up to a multiple of 64: 256. Short sequences mean little padding and fast training; most of each example is the system prompt, which is the price of training with the exact serving prompt.

On the Hub

Both datasets are private on the Hugging Face Hub with a dataset card (source, licence, triage, the rewrite prompt, split sizes, token stats). The licence stays CDLA-Sharing 1.0: it requires shared derivatives to keep the same licence.

11 · Phrasing probes: does messy wording hurt?

Decision 5 needs rude and messy test messages, and the 100-case golden set had no offensive ones and only 3–4 each with heavy typos, politeness or keyword-only phrasing. Adding cases there would change the set the baseline scores (25 and 58) were measured on, so 12 phrasing probes live apart in evals/probes/. Each rewords one golden case and keeps its customer, order and expected answer, which the validator enforces, so a probe that fails while its clean twin passes failed because of the wording.

On the rude frying-pan return, the 2B found the same order and status as for the clean message, then searched the policy instead of proposing the return, ran out of calls and handed off. The thing to watch after fine-tuning: rudeness leading to hedging instead of the action asked for. With 3 probes per phrasing, one case is the whole signal; the probes flag large gaps, they cannot measure small ones.

13 · Defending the design: interview questions

Why not train on all 26,872 rows?

Because they would teach the wrong things: 3,108 duplicates (most in cancel_order, which causes drift toward cancelling), essay-length replies, and invented procedures in half the rows. 5,276 clean rows that teach the right form beat 26,872 that teach bad habits.

Why rewrite instead of filtering for good replies?

There are almost none to keep: 76% are over the length limit and 48% contain placeholders. The form is good and the content is bad in nearly every row, so the content is removed and the form kept.

Why not fill the placeholders with your store's values?

That turns a generic invented procedure into a specific invented one, which is more convincing and still wrong. Facts must come from the help center and tools at answer time.

Why keep the redirect intents instead of dropping them?

Customers will ask Nova to delete accounts and send invoices. Dropping them leaves that behaviour untrained; keeping 100 each teaches a short, honest pointer.

How do you know the rewrites are good?

Three layers: nine automatic rules, a judge model tested on planted mistakes, and 200 pairs read by hand, which also measured the judge (it caught 3 of my 6 rejections, so a pattern check was added for what it misses). Plus phrase counts after every run, which found four habits the checks could not see.

Why trust an LLM judge at all?

Only as far as it was tested: 20 planted pairs before use, a hand review after. It is reliable on concrete invented facts and weak on vague claims, and the pipeline accounts for both.

Won't the fine-tune just copy Gemini's style?

Yes, which is why the style was audited: "I'd be glad" was in 31% of replies and was removed. The final data has no opening above 3.5%. The prediction to check: no stock phrase in more than about 5% of Nova's replies after training.

Why train only on the assistant's tokens?

Training on everything teaches the model to write the customer's side too, and a reply can end with "Customer: thanks, and…". With labels at -100 for every other token, only Nova's words count.

Why does the loss start after the think block?

Because the serving prompt already contains it. Training the block would teach the model to produce something it is never asked to produce. A test in CI checks the marker against the real template.

Why no tools in the training prompt?

The replies never call tools. Tools listed in every example and never used would teach the model to ignore them, which is the opposite of the next fine-tune's goal. The prompt otherwise matches serving exactly.

Why hold out two whole intents?

A random split tests fitting; unseen intents test transfer. If Nova handles payment_issue well without having seen it, it learned support behaviour, not templates.

Doesn't double-weighting rare phrasings bias the data?

It moves them back toward their natural share after dedup thinned them: keyword-only went from 4.7% in a plain sample to 6.9%, against 8.3% in the raw data. The goal is robustness to how real customers write.

How did you pick the sequence length?

Measured on the rendered examples: p99 245 tokens, max 255, so 256 (a multiple of 64) and nothing truncated.

What would you do differently?

Estimate the cost first. About ₹8–9k of credits went to the rewrite and judge, mostly thinking tokens. A free open model on a Kaggle GPU could have done the bulk rewrite, with the expensive model only judging a sample.

14 · Limits, what comes next, glossary

Known limits

  • One teacher's voice. All replies come from one model; its remaining style is the style Nova will learn.
  • The judge and the rules are not proof. 200 of 5,276 pairs were read; the rest rely on checks that were measured, not perfect.
  • Single-turn only. Nothing here teaches multi-turn behaviour or tool calls; that is the next dataset's job.
  • 12 probes is small. They flag a large wording effect, not a small one.

What comes next

  • SFT-1: LoRA fine-tuning of Qwen3.5-2B on nimbus-bitext-v1 at 256 tokens with the reply-only mask, measured against the floor on the same 100 golden cases and the 12 probes, and against the predictions in data/DECISIONS.md.
  • Later: retrieval over the help center, a proper tool layer, and multi-turn tool-using conversations built from nimbus-bitext-questions.
